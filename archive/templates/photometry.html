Extract photometric measurements around the specified sky point. The center field accepts sky coordinates or a resolvable object name, and the radius selects matching measurements around that position. Photometry searches default to 3 arcsec when no radius is provided, and submitted radii are limited to 5 arcmin.

Use the site, CCD, camera serial, filter, target ID, and night range fields to narrow the source images. Night values use YYYYMMDD. Leave quality cuts enabled for the cleaned light curve, or select Disable quality cuts to include all matching measurements.

Clip additionally rejects the measurements deviating from the median magnitude by more than the given number of median absolute deviations, taken separately for every camera and filter, as their zero points need not agree closely enough for a common median to mean anything. It is empty, and so disabled, on purpose: unlike the quality cuts, which reject what is known to be bad, this one rejects whatever deviates, and so removes the deep minima of a genuine variable together with the outliers. Useful for cleaning up a star expected to be constant, and best avoided otherwise.

Average close points combines the measurements of a single visit into one, which suits the target scripts that take several images in a row before moving on. Grouping is per site, camera and filter, so no average mixes zero points that need not agree, and within sets the largest gap still counted as the same visit; a group is never allowed to span more than three such intervals, so a long continuous sequence is binned rather than collapsed into a single point. The weighted mean is the most precise of the three combinations, shrinking the uncertainty roughly as the square root of the number of measurements, but also the most vulnerable to a spoiled one: a cosmic ray makes a measurement brighter, a brighter measurement is assigned a smaller error, a smaller error means a larger weight, and the average follows it. The median is immune to that at the price of a somewhat larger uncertainty, by a factor of about 1.25. The clipped mean is the compromise, dropping the measurements deviating from the median of their group before taking the weighted mean of the rest. Averaging also smears out variability faster than the window, so switch it off when looking for short periods.

Compute colors adds a second panel below the light curve, sharing its horizontal axis, with the B-V, V-R, V-I and R-I colors of the star. A color is the difference of two magnitudes measured on different frames, so the two have to be taken close enough in time for the star not to have changed between them: they are required to belong to the same night of the same camera, and then to be each other's nearest measurement no more than half a day apart. Matching is one to one, so three exposures in B against one in V make a single color rather than three sharing that V. The camera is part of the condition because the zero points of two of them need not agree closely enough for their difference to be a color of the star rather than one of the instruments. Colors follow the points they are made of, so the quality cuts, the clipping and the averaging all reach them, and the hover says how far apart the two measurements actually were.

The result view groups points by filter in an interactive light-curve plot with error bars and hover details. It also provides full and filtered text downloads, plus quick links to SIMBAD, AAVSO VSX, NSVS, ASAS-SN, and Aladin for the query position.

Search period computes a Lomb-Scargle periodogram of the very points displayed, down to the shortest period given beside it. Bands are pooled after centering each on its own median, as they sit whole magnitudes apart while the variability being looked for is far smaller. Clicking a peak folds the curve at that period; the dotted red lines mark the periods the observations themselves imprint on any periodogram — the day with its harmonics, the sidereal day and the year — and a peak sitting on one of those comes from the sampling rather than from the star. The solid green line is the period the curve is folded with, and the dashed green ones are the periods it would also appear at through the nightly sampling, at 1/(1/P ± n) per day: a real 0.8724 d signal necessarily produces peaks at 0.4659 and 6.8393 d as well, so a peak falling on a dashed line is that same period over again rather than a second one. The marks follow the Period field, so they move as soon as it is edited, and they are drawn whether or not the curve is actually folded. Each set of lines is named in the legend, and clicking its entry there hides it. The reported false alarm probability assumes pure noise and nothing else, so it is optimistic. Fold, Period and Epoch are applied in the browser, so editing them redraws immediately, and ÷2 / ×2 help with an eclipsing binary, whose periodogram peaks at half the true period.
